Deposits and Progress Payments for Trade Jobs

Updated July 2026 · Paper Trail Forms

A long job has a cash-flow shape that a short one does not. You buy the materials in week one, pay the labour every week after that, and if the whole invoice waits until the last screw goes in, you have financed the customer's project out of your own account for a month. Deposits and progress payments are how that gets fixed — not by charging more, but by charging in step with what you have already spent.

What a deposit is actually for

Two things, and it is worth being clear which one you are asking for because they justify different amounts.

The first is materials cover. If a job needs $3,000 of fittings before anyone turns a spanner, a deposit that covers those fittings is not a favour you are asking for — it is the customer paying for their own materials at the moment they are bought. This is the easiest deposit to justify, because you can show the supplier quote.

The second is commitment. A booked week that cancels on the Friday before is a week you cannot resell. A modest deposit makes the booking real. This one is harder to push high, and it is the reason a “50% up front” on a labour-only job meets more resistance than the same figure on a job that is mostly parts.

How much to ask for

There is no universal number, and anyone who gives you one is describing their own trade. What holds across trades is the logic:

  • Cover the materials, at minimum. If you would be out of pocket on day one, the deposit is too small.
  • Scale with the job length. A same-day call rarely needs a deposit at all. A three-week install almost always does.
  • Scale with the customer's exposure, not just yours. A first-time residential customer handing over a large sum to a stranger is taking a real risk. Smaller deposit, more frequent stages, and the trust gets built by the schedule rather than argued about up front.
  • Watch the consumer rules. Several jurisdictions cap deposits on home-improvement contracts, require deposits to be held in a particular way, or give a cooling-off period during which the customer can cancel. Check what applies where you work before you set a house rule.
Deposit limits, cooling-off rights, and the rules on holding customer money vary by country and by jurisdiction, and they are stricter for residential work than for commercial. Verify your local position — nothing here is legal advice.

Structuring progress payments

The mistake is to split by time (“a third a week”), because time keeps passing whether or not the work does, and a delayed job turns your payment schedule into an argument. Split by milestone instead: a stage is billable when something verifiable has happened.

A workable shape for a two-to-four week job:

StageTriggerRough share
DepositBooking confirmed, materials orderedMaterials cost + a little
Stage 1First fix complete and signed offA third of the balance
Stage 2Second fix complete and signed offA third of the balance
FinalSnagging done, job handed overThe remainder

Two details make this hold up. First, each trigger has to be something the customer can see — “first fix complete” is checkable, “60% done” is not. Second, sign-off should leave a record. A job sheet signed at the end of each stage turns “is stage two finished?” from an opinion into a document.

Keep the final payment worth having

The last stage is the one that gets disputed, because by then the customer has what they wanted and their leverage is at its highest. Two habits help.

Leave enough on the final invoice to be worth finishing for. If the closing payment is 5% of the job, snagging quietly drops down your priority list, and the customer notices. Ten to twenty percent keeps both sides motivated.

Invoice each stage the day it completes, not at the end. A stage invoice sent three weeks late reads as an afterthought and gets treated like one. Put it in writing before you start

Every part of this belongs in the estimate the customer accepts, not in a conversation on site. The deposit amount, what each stage means, what each stage costs, and what happens if the job stalls for a reason outside your control. An estimate that spells out the payment schedule is doing two jobs: winning the work, and pre-agreeing the thing most likely to go wrong later.

If a stage payment does go unpaid, stop before the next stage rather than working through it and hoping. Continuing is how a $2,000 problem becomes a $9,000 one, and it weakens your position rather than strengthening it. Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to ask for a deposit?

In the trades, yes, particularly on any job with material costs up front or more than a day of labour. What varies is the size and the wording, not whether it is reasonable to ask.

What if the customer refuses a deposit?

Ask what would make them comfortable. Often the objection is to the size or to paying a stranger, not to the principle — a smaller deposit with more frequent, smaller stages solves it. If nothing works and the job needs real money up front, that is useful information about the job.

Can I charge a late fee on an unpaid stage payment?

Only if your terms said so before the work started, and only within whatever your jurisdiction allows. A fee that appears for the first time on an overdue stage invoice is easy to dispute.

Do progress payments need separate invoices?

Yes. Each stage should be its own numbered invoice with its own date and due date, showing what stage it covers and what remains. A running total on one document gets confusing for both sides at tax time.
